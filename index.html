Mo3ta معطي

Palestine's statistics in one place: dashboards, a map and charts over a large Arabic dataset. A journalist, a researcher or a student should be able to find a number, see it in context and cite it.

Role
UX and UI design, then the whole build
Focus
Numbers in Arabic, map and chart components, server rendering
Built with
Vue 3 and Inertia with SSR, on Laravel
Live
mo3ta.ps

The decision. Keep everything around the data quiet so the map and the charts carry the colour, and never show a number without its date and its source.

Where the numbers were

Before this, the statistics lived in PDFs and spreadsheets. The centre wanted one place where a number could be found, seen on a map or a chart, and cited.

Why every page renders on the server

A figure that search engines cannot read is a figure nobody finds, so a client-only dashboard was ruled out early. Pages are rendered on the server with Inertia and arrive as real HTML, and Vue 3 adds the interactive layer on top.

One component set for every figure

The map and the charts are one reusable set, fed from the same indicator model, with export and share on each figure. They use the same tokens as the marketing pages, so the product and the site look like one thing.

Numbers, in Arabic

Figures sit on a monospaced scale so they line up in columns. The layout runs right to left throughout, and the axes and legends are placed so they still read naturally that way.

Result

  • Live at mo3ta.ps, with the indicator library, the map and the dashboards in production.

Devlo CMS ديفلو

One Laravel application runs 24 Arabic news sites, each with its own theme, modules, languages and editorial team. I designed the editorial panel and the theming model, then built both.

Role
Design and full-stack, product owner of the front-end
Focus
Theming model, a panel in three languages, guard rails
Built with
Blade, Livewire and Alpine on Laravel; MySQL, Redis
Demo
demo.taktek.co, behind a login

The decision. A site may differ in configuration, not in behaviour. Every newsroom runs the same modules and the same code; what changes is the theme, the languages and which modules are switched on.

One theme, from a six-page brand book: IWAD

  • Maroon#3D1720Primary surfaces and headings
  • Gold#A8824AThe mark only: squares, borders, large type
  • Gold for text#7E5F31Any gold text, 5.03:1 on paper
  • Paper#F0EDE6Editorial surfaces
  • Cream#F3EEEAText on maroon
  • Ink#1A1A1ABody text

IWAD, a defence research institute, came with a logo, clear space and six colours. The type scale, spacing, components and section colours were designed from those. The brand gold is 3.02:1 on paper, which fails for text, so it only marks things and a darker gold carries every gold word.

Twenty-four forks

Newsrooms used to be built one at a time, each a fork of the last. A fix in one site never reached the others, and every new client meant weeks of repeated work. Share previews show what that costs after a few years: each theme had grown its own share handler, and some fell back to a favicon, which WhatsApp will not turn into a card.

Modules, themes and switches

Front pages are built from shared modules: a breaking strip, a lead story, a headline rail, an audio briefing, podcast and translation sections. Editors switch them on per site. A new feature starts switched off everywhere and is turned on for one site in the same commit that writes it, so the question of who gets it is answered before it ships.

A theme from a brand book

IWAD is one of the themes: a research platform for a defence institute, 24 pages, right to left. The brand book was six pages and had no type scale, spacing or components, so those were designed first, in two written documents, and the pages were built on them. Sections are told apart by colour instead of icons, because anything military reads as combat.

A panel that shows only what a newsroom uses

The editorial panel works in Arabic, English and Turkish, right to left by default. Switching a module off removes its route, its sidebar entry and its permission together, so an editor never meets a menu item that leads nowhere. Every label is a translation key, 5,874 of them, checked in CI.

Small details, decided once

Share previews run through one controller for every site. The site name is not added to the title, because WhatsApp and Telegram give the title a single line and the prefix was eating the part of the headline that earns the click. Descriptions stop at 200 characters, which still reads as a full sentence on every platform.

Checks for what nobody can see

Some failures never throw an error: an English string that renders in Arabic because Arabic is the fallback, a share image a crawler quietly refuses, a permission that exists on one route and nowhere else. Each has a command that fails before a commit lands, across 456 Livewire components and 839 Blade views.

Measuring a live newsroom

Maktoob Media, the English site on the platform, felt slow on mobile. Lighthouse against production moved 20 points between identical runs, so before touching the site I built a rig that could tell two builds apart. Fonts went from 749 KB to 78 KB and icons from 302 KB to 31.6 KB. The usual advice, content-visibility on the off-screen sections, measured 84 against 83 and was thrown away.

Result

  • Runs 24 sites, among them Quds News Network, Maktoob Media, Tayaqan, Hodhod, Adeni Cast and the Saleh Al-Arouri archive.
  • Quds News Network, real Chrome users over 28 days: FAST, with LCP at 2.33 s and INP at 104 ms.

What I would do differently

The guard commands arrived after the failures they catch, one at a time, each written the day something silent reached a client. They should have come first for each part of the system. Writing the check first forces you to decide what correct means, and that was the part that was actually missing.

Kevla Campa

A marketing site for KEVLA CAMPA, a teardrop caravan built as one Kevlar and carbon-fibre shell. It is a design concept, not a launched site: I designed it and wrote all of it by hand, in HTML, CSS and JavaScript with no framework and no build step.

Role
Design and front-end, a concept
Focus
Scroll scenes that explain the shell, motion that stays smooth, accessibility checked
Built with
HTML, CSS and ES modules; no framework, no build step
Live demo
imsemoo.github.io/kevla-campa

The decision. The scroll does the explaining. The home page walks through the shell in four pinned scenes, from the laminate to the places it goes, and each scene is a CSS animation on its own view timeline, not a script listening to the scroll.

The system: a dark shell, one lime accent

  • Ground#07090AThe page, dark by default
  • Surface#0E1214Photo frames, tiles and the explorer
  • Light#EEF0EDThe light sections, set in ink
  • Lime#6BD117Links, focus and selection, 10.2:1 on the ground
  • Cyan#12B7C9Hover and section labels on the dark
  • Cyan on light#076570The same job on light sections, 5.9:1

Archivo carries everything large, widened to between 110 and 118 percent, and Azeret Mono carries the labels, never below 11 px. The cyan falls to 2.1:1 on the light sections, so they get a darker one, and every translucent colour is mixed from these tokens with color-mix().

Four scenes, one timeline each

The home page pins four scenes: the hero, a scan that turns raw laminate into the finished shell, a track through the places it goes, and a closing call to action. Each one is a keyframe animation on the view timeline of its section, so it runs off the main thread. Browsers without scroll timelines get a small script that seeks the same keyframes, and with reduced motion or JavaScript off the page is a plain, fully visible layout.

Measured before it was changed

The adventure track felt jerky. Rendering held 60 fps, with a 95th-percentile frame of 16.8 ms even at four times CPU throttling, so the fault was the motion itself: each move was squeezed into 12% of the scene and jumped up to 296 px in a frame. The track now rests on each panel and glides over 16% on a sine curve, which cut the jump to 73 px at 1440 px wide and 16 px on a phone, and no headline is ever shown clipped.

What the browser already does

The menu, the enquiry drawer and the gallery lightbox are native dialogs, so focus trapping and Escape come from the browser. They animate out as well as in, and pages crossfade in 220 ms with cross-document view transitions while the header holds still.

Photos that hold still

On the caravan page the hotspots stay locked to the photo at every size, and each one fills the detail panel with its own photo and notes. The next photo swaps in only once it has decoded, so the panel never flashes empty.

Placeholders that say so

The figures from the maker were not supplied, so none were invented: the specification sheet reads Awaiting client figures, the story copy is marked DUMMY, and the phone number sits in a range reserved for fiction. The README lists everything to replace before launch.

Fonts that do not move the page

Archivo and Azeret Mono are self-hosted variable fonts with fallbacks sized from the font files, so the text takes nearly the same space before and after the fonts arrive, and layout shift stays at 0 on every page.

Result

  • A design concept: not launched, and every stand-in on it is marked as one.
  • No axe-core violations across 24 states, every page at desktop and phone widths plus the open dialogs and form errors, and 100 for accessibility, best practices and SEO in Lighthouse on every page, from the runs recorded in the repository.
  • The code, the README and the six motion plans behind the changes are public.

What I would do differently

Put the buttons in the hero on screen sooner. They finish arriving at about 2.35 seconds; the motion audit in the repository noted it and left it for later, and a shorter, regular stagger would fix it.

OzCar

Used-car marketplace. Front-end, built from Adobe XD designs.

A used-car marketplace and an auction dashboard for an Australian dealer group. The designs came as Adobe XD files, and every screen had to become markup a back-end team could wire up without redrawing anything.

Notes from the build

  • Filters and calculators work as plain forms first, and jQuery adds the live behaviour on top.

How a project runs.

The same six steps for a landing page or a platform. Every step ends with something written down or measured.

  1. 01

    Brief

    You send the goal and whatever exists: content, a brand book, a Figma file, the current site. I send back questions in writing, so the decisions are on record for your team.

  2. 02

    Structure

    Flows, the page list and what each screen has to do. On Arabic and bilingual products this is also where right to left gets decided, before any layout.

  3. 03

    First screen

    One screen, designed and agreed before the rest exists. If the direction is wrong, we lose a day.

  4. 04

    Build

    Components first, then pages, delivered in stages you can review. The same person designs it and writes the code, so there is no handover to lose things in.

  5. 05

    Measure

    PageSpeed on both devices, accessibility checks and a pass at 375 px before anything is called finished. The tables below are this step, still running.

  6. 06

    Keep it running

    Most of my work lands in systems that already run. I read the existing code before I change it, and keep what works.
